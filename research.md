Dr. Ovalle’s research advances understanding of _heterogeneity_ in the nonprofit sector, examining how civic organizations serve as hubs of democratic expression, meet public and private needs, and manage the tensions inherent in balancing multiple responsibilities. Informed by professional service and scholarly inquiry, her work highlights the sector’s contributions while assessing its challenges and opportunities for improvement.

Her work engages with these interconnected areas of inquiry:  
- **Civil Society in Public Policy**  
- **Nonprofit and Philanthropic Finance**  
- **Climate and Democratic Governance Studies**

## Dissertation
**_Environmental Nonprofits in U.S. Climate Governance: Understanding Resources, Rhetoric, and Heterogeneity_**  
This dissertation research leverages network analysis, regression modeling, and large-scale text analysis to examine how environmental nonprofits' funding patterns, messaging strategies, and organizational diversity shape decision-making, climate priorities, and resource mobilization across the United States.



## Working Papers
**_Job Market Paper_**  
**A Heterogeneous, Not Monolithic, U.S. Environmental Nonprofit Sector as Evidenced by Variation in Climate Messaging**  
Given growing IRS scrutiny, lobbying restrictions, and state-level limits on advocacy, many nonprofits are navigating whether and how to address societal challenges tied to contested policy agendas. This tension is especially visible in the U.S. environmental nonprofit sector. Though often perceived as a cohesive progressive sector, little systematic analysis has assessed how much this cohesion actually holds, or how much variance exists in the work ENPOs articulate. As a result, the sector risks over- or undergeneralization of its reach, scope, and governance role. This study explores this information gap. Using 2023 990 tax, financial, and text data, often mined for individual cases used to generalize about the sector, we instead analyze this data in aggregate across a robust population of ENPOs. We combine regression analysis with large scale text analysis to examine climate related messaging among more than 10,000 ENPOs across 25 nonprofit subsectors. We assess how organizational characteristics, financial capacity, and community context correspond with variation in climate focus. We find that only 29 percent of organizations explicitly reference climate change or social justice in their mission statements or program descriptions, and stronger financial performance corresponds with a lower likelihood of such messaging. Topic modeling of mission and program text also shows distinct clusters of environmental focus that vary systematically across organizational structures and communities. These findings point to a heterogeneous, not monolithic, ENPO sector, offering insight into how nonprofits calibrate advocacy amid mounting legal and political constraints, and underscore the importance of systematic analysis in countering politicized narratives of the nonprofit sector.
